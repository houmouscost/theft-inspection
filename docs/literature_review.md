# Literature study: inspecting under adaptation

The paper's empirical core is defensible, but its framing under-cites three bodies of work that a Trans. Smart Grid referee is likely to know. The first is adversarial-evasion research on theft detectors, much of it in this journal. The second is earlier game-theoretic models of electricity theft. The third, the most consequential for the theory, is the economics of tax auditing, which already contains our deterrence condition and predicts the structure of our learned inspection policy. Engaging with these turns Propositions 1–3 from free-standing claims into instances of known results with a new twist (ML scores as audit signals, budgeted inspection, adaptive depth). It also gives the strategy-aware policy a theoretical anchor rather than a purely numerical one. Four literature-driven diagnostics on the existing data point to concrete analysis upgrades, and one of them (the finite-sample check of Proposition 1) is needed before submission.

## Positioning in the theft-detection literature

### Detectors, labels and SGCC

Supervised detection on smart-meter data is a mature field. Reviews organise it into data-oriented, network-oriented and hybrid schemes ([Messinis & Hatziargyriou 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.epsr.2018.01.005); [Viegas et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rser.2017.05.193); [Glauner et al. 2017a](https://doi.org/10.2991/ijcis.2017.10.1.51)), and SGCC became the default benchmark through the Wide&Deep CNN of [Zheng et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1109/tii.2017.2785963). The strongest industrial studies train on labels produced by field inspections, for example roughly 57,000 Endesa inspections in [Buzau et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/tsg.2018.2807925), and the NTL community has long warned that such labels are a selected, non-representative sample of customers ([Glauner et al. 2017b](https://doi.org/10.1109/isap.2017.8071420); [Glauner et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1142/9789813273238_0057)). This literature matters for our headline result. Our detectors learn that high consumption is suspicious, and a selection mechanism in which utilities inspect large consumers would produce exactly that label pattern. In SGCC the labelled-theft rate among consumers with positive load rises from 3.1% in the lowest consumption decile to 22.2% in the highest (our computation), and it also rises with the missing-data fraction, from 6.8% to 12.0% across quintiles. That second pattern matches [Fei et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijepes.2021.107410), who report that the location of missing values carries strong theft signal. The paper currently explains the inversion as a property of thieves; the literature supports presenting it as at least partly a property of how labels were generated. That reading strengthens the argument for evaluating against modelled adaptation instead of trusting real-label AUC.

A second caution comes from [Altamimi et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.energy.2025.137763), the first evaluation of synthetic attack models against real labelled theft. Models trained on synthetic attacks may fail on real theft cases, and method rankings on synthetic attacks need not carry over. This cuts both ways for us. It is a threat to validity for S1–S3, which use synthetic under-reporting on honest consumers. It also independently corroborates our finding that real-label skill and simulated-attack skill diverge. Distribution shift across utilities ([Liao et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/tpwrs.2024.3375939)) is the related problem for our UCI replication.

Three properties of SGCC itself deserve a sentence in the protocol (our computation). First, 2,114 consumers have all-zero series, 36 of them labelled thieves, whose label cannot be explained by consumption. Second, 1,009 non-empty rows are exact copies of another row, but almost all are all-zero series that share a missingness pattern: only two groups (four rows) with positive consumption are duplicated, and four groups carry conflicting labels. Third, the file is sorted with all labelled thieves first, so unshuffled cross-validation would fail silently.

### Evasion and poisoning attacks on theft detectors

The adversarial-ML line is the closest empirical neighbour, and a referee will ask how we differ from it. [Li et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1109/smartgridcomm47815.2020.9303013) showed that crafted measurements let a thief report extremely low consumption while bypassing neural detectors, and [Li et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3433210.3437513) generalised this to constrained attacks on cyber-physical systems. In Trans. Smart Grid, [Takiddin et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1109/tsg.2022.3193989) report detection degradation of up to 35.8%, 26.9% and 22.2% under white-, gray- and black-box evasion and propose an ensemble anomaly detector trained on benign data. The same group studied label poisoning ([Takiddin et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1109/tsg.2020.3047864)). Closest to our A3 attack, [Badr et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1109/jiot.2023.3243086) describe a high-consumption consumer who reports a GAN-generated low-consumption profile, with 82–97% success against global detectors. Their countermeasure is one detector per consumption-level cluster ([Elgarhy et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1109/access.2023.3318111) add clustering and ensembles). [Wang et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/comst.2023.3344808) survey evasion in cyber-physical systems more broadly.

None of these papers models the thief's economic trade-off between theft depth, detection and fines, the inspection budget, or randomised allocation. They measure detection-rate degradation under a perturbation budget. That is the clean differentiator to state in the introduction: evasion work asks whether a sample is misclassified, while we ask how much energy is recovered under a published, budgeted inspection policy when thieves best-respond. Badr et al.'s countermeasure also maps onto our S3 result. Per-cluster detection only resists level mimicry if cluster membership comes from something the thief cannot report, such as pre-theft history or contracted capacity. That is exactly the history-based signal that makes change-aware detection work in S3.

### Game-theoretic theft models

The paper currently cites no earlier game model of electricity theft, and two exist that it must engage. [Cardenas et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1109/allerton.2012.6483444) formulate theft detection as a game in which the thief steals a predefined amount while minimising detection likelihood, and the utility trades detection probability against operational cost. [Amin et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1109/mcs.2014.2364711) develop this line in IEEE Control Systems. [Lin et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1109/tsg.2014.2327809) is a two-page Trans. Smart Grid letter that applies a non-cooperative game to NTL screening, [Wei et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1109/rweek.2017.8088640) combine Benford's law with a Stackelberg game, and [Liu et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/en18226059) use contract theory to trade privacy against detection. Our model differs in three ways: theft depth is chosen rather than fixed, the policy is a budgeted randomised allocation built on ML scores, and evaluation is out of sample on real smart-meter data. The related-work section should say so explicitly.

## Theory foundations

### Enforcement and audit economics

The thief who under-reports metered consumption is the taxpayer who under-reports income, and the tax-compliance literature solved our deterrence problem fifty years ago. [Allingham & Sandmo 1972](https://doi.org/10.1016/0047-2727%2872%2990010-2) model a taxpayer who chooses declared income given an audit probability and a proportional penalty ([Yitzhaki 1974](https://doi.org/10.1016/0047-2727%2874%2990037-1) refines the penalty base). In the risk-neutral case the choice is a corner solution: full evasion unless the audit probability times one plus the penalty rate reaches one. That is our Proposition 3, which also follows from [Becker 1968](https://doi.org/10.1086/259394) and is surveyed by [Polinsky & Shavell 2000](https://doi.org/10.1257/jel.38.1.45) and [Slemrod 2019](https://doi.org/10.1257/jel.20181437). Presenting Proposition 3 as this known corner result is more honest and costs nothing.

The more valuable connection is to optimal audit rules under commitment. With a committed audit policy, [Reinganum & Wilde 1985](https://doi.org/10.1016/0047-2727%2885%2990035-0) obtain a cutoff rule that audits reports below a threshold, and [Border & Sobel 1987](https://doi.org/10.2307/2297481) show that optimal audit probabilities decrease in the reported amount. [Sánchez & Sobel 1993](https://doi.org/10.1016/0047-2727%2893%2990091-7) extend this to hierarchical designs, and [Reinganum & Wilde 1986](https://doi.org/10.2307/2526692) analyse the case without commitment. Scotchmer's audit classes (AER 1987) condition audit rates on observable characteristics, and [Macho–Stadler & Pérez–Castrillo 2002](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0335.00268) let the auditor observe a noisy signal of true income before deciding, which is precisely the role of an ML score. [Scotchmer & Slemrod 1989](https://doi.org/10.1016/0047-2727%2889%2990009-1) show that randomness in enforcement itself affects compliance, and [Erard & Feinstein 1994](https://doi.org/10.2307/2555850) add intrinsically honest taxpayers. Our learned policy targets low-but-not-minimal reported level and largely ignores the score. That is a Reinganum–Wilde cutoff rule truncated at the bottom: near-zero honest consumers make the lowest band too expensive to cover within the budget. The inspection-games literature ([Avenhaus et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1016/s1574-0005%2802%2903014-x)) provides the same structure from the verification side.

### Strategic classification and randomisation

The ML side of the theory is strategic classification. It began with adversarial classification ([Dalvi et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1145/1014052.1014066)) and Stackelberg prediction games ([Brückner & Scheffer 2011](https://doi.org/10.1145/2020408.2020495)), and was formalised by [Hardt et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2840728.2840730). Two results bear directly on our policy family. [Braverman & Garg 2020](https://doi.org/10.4230/LIPIcs.FORC.2020.9) prove that randomised classifiers are necessary for efficiency under strategic manipulation, and often induce agents not to manipulate at all. They also show that randomisation is not a stable best response for the classifier, a commitment problem that matches our assumption of a published, committed policy. [Rota Bulo et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1109/tnnls.2016.2593488) make the same point for randomised prediction games in security applications. For policies rather than classifiers, [Tsirtsis et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2021.02567) show that optimal decision policies under strategic behaviour are hard to compute in general and that deterministic policies can be suboptimal. This justifies both our randomised cell policies and the heuristic search. Top-$k$ inspection is a rank-based allocation with competition through the threshold, which is the setting of [Liu et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.08240). Who leads and who follows depends on relative update speeds ([Zrnic et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.12529)), which is the right way to discuss when the Stackelberg assumption holds. [Kleinberg & Raghavan 2020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3417742), [Milli et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1145/3287560.3287576), [Miller et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10362) and [Haghtalab et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.24963/ijcai.2020/23) supply the vocabulary of gaming versus improvement, social cost and incentive-aware evaluation. [Perdomo et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.06673) frame retraining on induced distributions.

### Security games

Committing to a randomised allocation against best-responding attackers is the Stackelberg security-game paradigm ([Tambe 2011](https://doi.org/10.1017/cbo9780511973031); [Sinha et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.24963/ijcai.2018/775)). Computing the optimal commitment against many attacker types is NP-hard ([Conitzer & Sandholm 2006](https://doi.org/10.1145/1134707.1134717)), which motivates mixed-integer formulations (Paruchuri et al. 2008) and compact representations (Kiekintveld et al. 2009, AAMAS). Two refinements are directly usable. Behavioural adversary models such as quantal response ([McKelvey & Palfrey 1995](https://doi.org/10.1006/game.1995.1023)) and SUQR ([Nguyen et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v27i1.8599)) generalise our E9. Learning the attacker model online ([Balcan et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1145/2764468.2764478)) is the natural extension when thief types are unknown. One more connection is useful. In single-type security games the optimal coverage typically equalises attacker utility across the targets that are attacked. Our learned band of roughly equal inspection probabilities is the same phenomenon, and saying so would make the policy figure interpretable.

### Detectability and certificates

Proposition 1 is a two-point testing bound. Its power-systems relatives are unobservable false-data injection ([Liu et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1145/1952982.1952995); [Kosut et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1109/tsg.2011.2163807)) and stealthiness defined through a divergence between attacked and nominal output distributions ([Bai et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.automatica.2017.04.047)). Its security relative is the mimicry attack on intrusion detectors ([Wagner & Soto 2002](https://doi.org/10.1145/586110.586145)). Phrasing Proposition 1 as "$\epsilon$-stealthiness in level space" connects it to this literature, and Pinsker's inequality gives a KL version for free. For the certificates, split conformal ([Lei et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.2017.1307116)) assumes exchangeability. Weighted conformal handles covariate shift ([Tibshirani et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06019)), and [Barber et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1214/23-aos2276) bound the coverage loss under arbitrary non-exchangeability by a weighted total-variation term. [Fannjiang et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2204569119) treat the case where the deployed model itself shifts the data, which is our adaptive deployment. [Gibbs & Candès 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.00170) provide online recalibration. The learning-augmented algorithms framing ([Lykouris & Vassilvitskii 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3447579); Purohit et al. 2018; [Mitzenmacher & Vassilvitskii 2022](https://doi.org/10.1145/3528087)) is the right home for the robustness–consistency language of Proposition 2.

## Recommendations

### Theory

1. Recast Proposition 3 as the risk-neutral Allingham–Sandmo/Becker corner solution and cite inspection games. This removes any appearance of claiming a known result as new.
2. Add a proposition on the structure of the optimal cell policy against static and adaptive thieves, framed as a Reinganum–Wilde cutoff truncated by the honest mass at low levels. For static thieves the optimum is a fractional knapsack over cells ranked by recoverable theft per unit of inspection. For adaptive thieves, state the security-game indifference property and check it against the learned policies.
3. Restate Proposition 1 as level-space stealthiness, with a KL corollary via Pinsker, and cite Badr et al. as the empirical precedent for level mimicry.
4. Turn E5 into a bound. Barber et al.'s coverage-gap result predicts the certificate failure from the total-variation distance between calibration and deployment miss probabilities, so the observed collapse becomes a quantitative check instead of an anecdote.
5. Justify the commitment assumption with Braverman & Garg (randomisation needs commitment) and Zrnic et al. (order of play), and state when it may fail.

### Analyses

1. **Finite-sample E4 (required).** Our histogram TV compares two different samples and is biased upwards; a kernel-density shift estimate is tighter (bound 0.528 at $r{=}0.1$ and 0.856 at $r{=}0.9$, against 0.556 and 0.862 now). The empirical balanced accuracy is a maximum over thresholds on about 659 thieves and is also biased upwards: at $r{=}0.1$, A3 reaches 0.537 against a kernel bound of 0.522–0.528, depending on bandwidth. Estimate TV by kernel density of honest levels against their own shift, and calibrate the KS statistic with a permutation null or a DKW band. Without this, a referee can point to an apparent violation at small $r$.
2. **Label-selection diagnostic.** Add the theft-rate-by-decile and missingness-quintile table to the paper. It supports the selection-bias reading of the inversion.
3. **SGCC data hygiene.** Report the all-zero consumers, labelled thieves with no consumption, duplicate groups and file ordering, and confirm that results are unchanged when zero-load consumers are excluded from the real-label analysis as well.
4. **History-anchored per-cluster detectors in S3.** Implement Badr et al.'s countermeasure with clusters defined by pre-onset level versus reported level. This shows directly that the countermeasure needs a reference the thief cannot manipulate.
5. **Interpretable policy.** Fit a three-parameter interval rule (inspect reported level in $[a,c]$ with probability $p$) and compare it with the full cell policy. If it recovers most of the gain, it gives utilities a deployable rule and matches the audit-cutoff theory.
6. **Attack families.** Map A1–A4 onto the synthetic attack families commonly used since Jokar et al. 2016, and add one gradient-based evasion attack in the style of Li et al. 2020 or Takiddin et al. 2023 as an extra thief option in E7. This shows that the economic best response dominates or complements perturbation attacks.
7. **E5 coverage gap.** Compute the total-variation distance between static and adaptive miss-probability pools and compare it with the observed coverage loss.
